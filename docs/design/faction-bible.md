# Commanders in Chief — Faction Character Bible

**Status:** working draft. Project name and faction short-names are provisional.
**Scope:** faction character only. No plot, no named characters, no mission
structure. This document exists so that any writer or LLM producing briefing
text, unit barks, UI copy, or mission framing produces output that sounds like
it came from the same world.

**Companion document, not yet written:** `cic-audio-video-style.md` will cover
presentation — bark families, voice generation and locking, radio processing per
faction, and the three diegetic footage formats. Character lives here; delivery
will live there.

---

## 0. How to use this document

Read the **Global Register** section first — it applies to everything. Then read
the section for the faction you are writing, then the **Lexicon** to check how
that faction refers to the others.

Two hard rules:

1. **Write from inside the faction.** Every faction believes it is the
   responsible party in this war. None of them are lying about that. If your
   text reads as though the faction knows it is the villain, it is wrong.
2. **Never resolve the moral question.** The setting has no correct side. Do not
   write a line whose purpose is to tell the audience who to root for.

---

## 1. Global Register

The tone target is *adult*, meaning: restrained, procedural, and quietly
appalling. It is not "dark." Darkness is cheap and reads as adolescent just as
fast as comedy does.

**Do:**

- Use real communications discipline. Brevity, call-and-response, flat delivery.
  Terrible things are reported in the same voice as fuel states.
- Let institutions cause harm through process rather than malice. A form filed
  correctly should be able to kill people.
- Show competent people constrained by rules, budgets, politics and time.
- Prefer understatement. The worst events in the setting should be described in
  the fewest words.
- Allow humour, but only the kind soldiers actually use: dry, terse, defensive,
  and never aimed at the audience.

**Do not:**

- Write joke unit barks, catchphrases, or mascot voices.
- Write a faction leader who monologues about their philosophy.
- Use suicide attacks, civilian death, or atrocity as a punchline or a gimmick.
- Editorialise. If a briefing orders something ugly, the briefing does not
  comment on it. Let the player sit with it.
- Escalate toward a revealed larger enemy. There is no third party who makes the
  war retroactively simple. Ambiguity stays ambiguous.

**The moral geometry.** Each faction must have: a defensible internal logic, a
constituency it genuinely serves, a real thing it is wrong about, and an
internal opposition that thinks the current course is a mistake. A faction
missing any of these four is under-written.

## 2. The Three Factions at a Glance

| | **AEC** | **Concord** | **Meridian** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full form | Allied Expeditionary Command | Continental Concord | Meridian Council |
| Colour *(campaign)* | blue — cold, printed, institutional | green — deep, painted, permanent | yellow — high-visibility, brushed on |
| Names itself after | a command structure | a civilisation | a line of connection |
| Claim to legitimacy | mandate, legality, coalition consent | order, continuity, development | consent of the people on the road |
| Sees the war as | a stabilisation problem | a continuity problem | a recognition problem |
| Sees itself as | the reluctant adult | the patient adult | the only local adult |
| Core fear | attribution and political collapse at home | disorder spreading inward | irrelevance the moment the road is legal |
| Institutional metaphor | a task force | a public works ministry | a supply chain with a seal |
| Time horizon | one rotation | one generation | this season |
| What it cannot admit | that it is a foreign army | that order is being imposed | that it needs the war |

The contrast is the point. One faction names itself after a filing structure,
one after a civilisation, one after the line that connects them. Meridian does
not name a territory it owns. It names the road, exchange, and dependence
between larger powers. That tells you what each thinks it is before a single
line of dialogue.

**Colour is a campaign system.** In the campaign the three colours above are
canonical: insignia, map markers, briefing plates and UI accents all key off
them, and a player learns to read the war by colour before reading a label. In
skirmish and multiplayer the player picks a colour instead, unique within the
game, because a match can field two AEC seats and ownership has to stay legible
where faction does not distinguish it. The two systems answer different
questions — the campaign colour says *who this is*, the skirmish colour says
*which one is mine* — and neither derives from the other. Neither is allowed to
silently stand in for the other either: a campaign mission must not renumber its
factions into seat colours, and a skirmish must not quietly hand a player the
colour of the faction they picked.

Keep Concord and Meridian separated by **value**, not hue. Green and yellow are
the pair that collapses under the common colour-vision deficiencies, so Concord
sits dark and Meridian sits bright, and the same rule constrains whatever palette
skirmish offers. A set of colours that reads as three factions only for players
with unimpaired colour vision has failed at the one job colour has here.

## 3. Lexicon — who calls whom what

Belligerents do not agree on each other's names. This costs nothing and does
enormous tonal work. Never let a faction use another faction's preferred term.

| Speaker | Refers to AEC as | Refers to Concord as | Refers to Meridian as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **AEC** | "the coalition", "Command" | "Concord forces", "the Concord" | "irregulars", "hostile elements", "the Group" |
| **Concord** | "the coalition" (with contempt), "the expedition" | "the Concord", "we" | "the bandit administration", "the tax gangs" |
| **Meridian** | "the foreigners", "the airfield" | "the roadbuilders", "the company" | "Meridian", "the Council", "the Chain" |

Additional register notes:

- AEC speaks in acronyms and euphemism: *kinetic activity*, *collateral
  assessment*, *shaping operations*, *permissive environment*.
- Concord speaks in infrastructure and duty: *the corridor*, *continuity*,
  *the schedule*, *rectification*, *the province*.
- Meridian speaks in administration: *permit*, *manifest*, *duty*, *the
  register*, *cleared*, *held pending*. The Council is polished and speaks for
  publication. The Chain is terse, local, and unconcerned with presentation.

A useful test: if you swapped the speaker attribution on a line, would anyone
notice? If not, rewrite it.

## 4. AEC — Allied Expeditionary Command

### Self-conception

A coalition that did not want this and cannot leave. It believes it is holding
open something the world needs, at cost to itself, on behalf of people who will
resent it either way. Its officers are professionally excellent and politically
exhausted. It measures success in rotations survived, not ground taken.

Its legitimacy claim is *procedural*: mandates, resolutions, contributing
nations, rules of engagement. This is sincere. It is also the mechanism by which
it does terrible things with clean paperwork.

### What it is wrong about

That legality and legitimacy are the same thing. It genuinely cannot understand
why a population it is protecting under a lawful mandate treats it as an
occupier. Its documents are all in order. It expects that to matter.

### Voice

Bureaucratic euphemism over enormous violence. Everything is a *package*, an
*asset*, a *window*, an *effect*. Briefings are structured, sourced and
caveated. Radio traffic is clipped and professional. Emotional content leaks
out sideways: a pause, a repeated readback, a request for confirmation that
nobody needed.

Contributing-nation contingents do not sound identical. Different ROE, different
appetite for risk, occasional refusals politely worded.

### Doctrine and mechanical identity

**Production as logistics.** Force arrives from off-map. Pads, beacons, drop
zones rather than factories — thin footprint, fast to establish, relocatable,
and dependent on air corridors staying open. Repair is fast, drone-mediated and
expensive. Vision is *purchased*: ISR sweeps and satellite windows on cooldown,
so AEC habitually fights with better information than anyone else.

Strengths: reaction speed, precision, information, mobility.
Weaknesses: air denial is near-existential; every loss is expensive; the whole
apparatus degrades badly under EW and GPS denial.

### Aesthetic

Clean, modular, faintly temporary. Airfields, container walls, gravel pads,
prefab. Nothing built to last, because it isn't meant to. Everything stencilled,
numbered and inventoried. The camp looks like it could be gone in seventy-two
hours, and everyone knows it.

Its blue arrives *printed* rather than painted: stencils, placards, lanyards and
the header of a form. A cold, desaturated grey-blue, the blue of a procurement
catalogue and an identity badge, and never heraldic — nothing here is claiming
ancestry. It is applied by a machine that also numbered the thing it is on.

### Internal fault lines *(for CiC 2)*

- **Theatre command vs. the political authority behind it.** The people fighting
  the war and the people who have to survive its coverage want different things
  and cannot say so on an open channel.
- **The targeting apparatus.** Whoever certifies a strike as lawful holds real
  power over whoever wants it fired. This is a genuine internal power centre and
  it is resented.
- **Contributing contingents.** National caveats, divergent ROE, and the quiet
  arithmetic of which flag takes which casualties.
- **The contractor layer.** Logistics, carriage and security are substantially
  privately run. Their incentives are not the coalition's, and the coalition's
  dependence on them is not fully documented anywhere.
- **The doctrinal split:** those who want the war fought cleanly and those who
  want it fought to an end, each convinced the other is getting people killed.

## 5. Concord — Continental Concord

### Self-conception

A civilisational project, not an army. It sees itself as the party that builds
things and the only one thinking in generations. It regards AEC as tourists with
firepower and Meridian as weather — a condition to be engineered around
rather than an enemy with a case.

Its legitimacy claim is *delivered outcomes*: the road exists, the power stays
on, the schedule holds. It considers this a stronger claim than any mandate, and
it is not obviously wrong.

### What it is wrong about

That order given is the same as order wanted. It cannot conceive that people
would prefer their own bad administration to a good one imposed, and it reads
that preference as a defect to be corrected rather than a position to be
negotiated with.

### Voice

Formal, patient, faintly civilisational. Speaks in schedules, quotas, phases and
duty. Setbacks are *irregularities*; opposition is *disorder*; brutality is
*rectification*. Language of harmony and connection deployed over enormous
coercive weight — and the speakers believe it.

Concord does not boast. It states what will be true by a given date.

### Doctrine and mechanical identity

**Production as industry.** Few very large factories, parallel queues, batch
delivery — a platoon arrives at once rather than a unit at a time. Slow to spin
up, then a rolling wave that does not stop. Builds permanent infrastructure
outward: depots, repair points, graded roads that speed movement. Its map
presence is literally paved.

Strengths: mass, staying power, engineering, attrition tolerance.
Weaknesses: turning radius. Enormous committed force, poor at reacting to
anything it did not plan for.

### Aesthetic

Heavy, permanent, deliberately impressive. Poured concrete, rail, gantries,
substations, banners with schedules on them. Everything looks like it intends to
still be there in fifty years, because that is the argument. Where AEC stencils
serial numbers, Concord carves completion dates.

Its green is deep and laid on thick: ministry signage, rolling stock, painted
structural steel, recoated so many times the edges have gone soft. Dark enough
to read as weight rather than growth — this is institutional green, not
landscape — and dark by requirement, since Meridian's yellow is what it has to
stay separable from.

### Internal fault lines *(for CiC 2)*

- **Civil authority vs. theatre command.** The war is a project with a budget
  and a political owner. The soldiers running it are answerable to people who
  have never been on the corridor.
- **Provincial and regional commands.** Several have their own industrial base,
  their own patronage and their own view of how much of this war they should be
  paying for.
- **The internal security organ.** Watches the army, is loathed by it, and holds
  files on everyone. Its interests are institutional survival, not victory.
- **Industrial combines.** Reconstruction contracts are the actual prize for
  some participants. A finished corridor ends the contracts.
- **The doctrinal split:** continuity versus decision. One camp wants to outlast
  everyone, absorbing losses on a generational clock. The other wants a single
  decisive stroke and considers patience a slow-motion defeat.

## 6. Meridian — Meridian Council

### Self-conception

Not an army. A *council for the road*. It issues transit permits, stamps
manifests, runs courts, hears disputes and collects duties. It does not want to
conquer the corridor; it wants passage to be conditional and itself to be the
condition. It regards both other factions as foreign parties negotiating over
a connection neither can use alone.

Its legitimacy claim is *presence and consent*: it was here, it is here, it
settles disputes people actually bring to it. That claim is partly true, which
is what makes it dangerous.

The name is deliberately non-territorial. A meridian is a line of relation: it
connects distant points without claiming to be either endpoint. The faction
occupies the same position between AEC and Concord. It is smaller than both,
but the road, supply chain, records and local institutions joining them pass
through it.

**Naming register:**

- **Meridian Council** is the formal name used on seals, notices and claims to
  legal standing.
- **Meridian** is the ordinary name for the faction.
- **The Council** is its civil-facing wing: courts, permits, registers,
  negotiators and public notices.
- **The Chain** is its distributed material wing: route-holders, haulers,
  workshops, suppliers, salvage crews and armed road groups. The name means
  both a chain of local nodes and the supply chain that keeps them useful.

> **Craft note.** A faction that hands you paperwork before it kills you is far
> more unsettling than a warlord with a rocket. Lead with the bureaucracy. The
> violence is the enforcement arm of a filing system.

### What it is wrong about

That it can become legitimate without ceasing to be what the war made it. The
Council wants legal standing and understands that standing eventually means
stopping. The Chain is the war economy: peace, standardized passage and lawful
accounting would dismantle the arrangements that keep it alive. Meridian can
become what it claims to be only by losing the machinery that made the claim
enforceable.

### Voice

Administrative, courteous, and unhurried. Permits, duties, registers, schedules
of fees. Threats phrased as procedure — *your manifest is held pending review*.
Never raises its voice. Uses "we" to mean the road and the people on it.

The two wings sound different. The Council is polished and speaks for
publication. The Chain is terse, local, and does not care how it sounds.

### Doctrine and mechanical identity

**Production as occupation.** No factories. Existing map structures — garages,
grain silos, apartment blocks, workshops — are converted into production. Build
capacity is a function of what is standing nearby, which forces the other
factions into the question of whether to level a town to kill a drone shop.

**Economy: taxed throughput.** Holds route nodes and earns from traffic passing
through, including the enemy's. This is the Chain in its most literal form:
every workshop, weighbridge, hauler and permit desk is one link. Optional bonus
income in contested places, never a gate on the other factions' economies —
greed, not homework.

**Army: captured, not built.** Salvage is doctrine, not a bonus. Recovered
hulls of AEC and Concord equipment, fielded at degraded reliability, keeping the
original silhouette. Their force reads visually as *this war has been going on a
long time* — and it is the engine of misidentification.

**Defence: concealment, not armour.** Tunnels, camouflage, false signatures,
illegal grid taps and generators. Terrifying while unobserved, brittle once
fixed in place. Where the others buy vision, Meridian buys ambiguity.

**Mass: cheap drones.** Small-workshop loitering munitions in quantity. Where a
human-delivered charge appears, it is rare, expensive, and treated on the radio
as a catastrophe — never as a mechanic and never as a joke.

Strengths: concealment, cheapness, local knowledge, attrition of patience.
Weaknesses: no heavy armour of its own; anything serious must be taken from
someone else; collapses fast once observed and pinned.

### Aesthetic

Improvised but *administrative*. Weighbridges, barriers, painted route numbers,
a desk in a shipping container with a stamp on it. Salvaged vehicles wearing two
sets of markings, one badly painted over. Order asserted with paint and paper
rather than concrete.

Its yellow is high-visibility and cheap: barrier paint, hazard chevrons,
weighbridge kerbs, the yellow of a permit and a hi-vis vest. It is the only
faction colour applied *over* another's. A recovered hull carries Meridian
yellow brushed across AEC blue or Concord green, and the covered coat still
shows at the panel edges and the door seams. That is the misidentification
engine in paint, and it should never be tidied up: the faction that asserts
order with a brush is the faction whose markings are always one coat thick.

### Financing

A state sponsor, laundered through freight companies. The load-bearing fact for
later plot work: **both** AEC and Concord have funded Meridian, at different
times, for different reasons. Reference obliquely; do not explain.

### Internal fault lines *(for CiC 2)*

- **The Council vs. the Chain.** The Council wants recognition and understands
  that recognition means eventually stopping. The Chain joins the workshops,
  haulers, route crews, local suppliers and armed protection that make passage
  possible. It is both a supply chain and the war economy, and cannot survive
  peace in its present form. Every irregular administration approaching
  statehood has this fight, and the war economy usually wins.
- **Regional route-holders.** Individual sections of road are held by groups
  with their own kin networks and their own idea of the correct duty rate.
  Central taxation is theoretical in several districts.
- **The sponsor's handlers.** Foreign money arrives with conditions. Whoever
  controls the relationship controls the movement, and everyone knows it.
- **The media arm.** Produces Meridian's legitimacy. Its interests diverge
  from the fighters' whenever the truth is inconvenient in the wrong direction.
- **The courts.** The judicial function is the most genuinely popular thing the
  Council does, and the most constrained by the Chain's needs.

## 7. Cross-faction constants

- **Everybody wants the road open.** All three. The war exists because "open" has
  three incompatible definitions. Never write a faction that benefits from
  closure — except parts of the Chain, and that is a secret, not a premise.
- **Nobody is fighting for territory.** They are fighting over the terms of
  passage.
- **Everyone has funded Meridian.** At some point, for some reason.
- **Every faction has a wing that wants this to stop** and is losing the
  argument to a wing that cannot afford to.
- **Every faction covers every tactical role.** Divergence is in *how* a role is
  filled, never in whether it exists. This is a writing constraint too: no
  faction is allowed to be characterised as helpless against something.
- **No faction is winning.** At any point in either game, all three can
  credibly believe they are one season from a favourable settlement.

## 8. Game split

### CiC 1 — between factions

Faction-specific campaigns. Each shows a coherent institution pursuing a
defensible goal and generating catastrophe. The player is competent, obedient,
and complicit. Antagonism is external; internal dissent is present but
subordinate — the friction of a handler and a commander who disagree, orders
that arrive with no comment attached, consequences that cross missions.

Character work stays at institutional scale. Voices are roles, not personalities.

### CiC 2 (or 1.5) — inside factions

The fault lines listed above become the plot. Struggles for control of a wing,
an organ, a budget, a route, a relationship with a sponsor — occasionally
lethal, usually procedural. The external war continues and is now the terrain
rather than the subject.

Tonal shift, deliberate: CiC 1 is about what institutions do to other people.
CiC 2 is about what they do to their own. The register gets quieter, closer, and
more personal without becoming melodramatic — the threat is usually a transfer,
an audit, a withheld shipment, or a door that doesn't open, and only sometimes a
rifle.

Constraint: CiC 2 must not retroactively explain CiC 1. No hidden hand, no
reveal that reduces the first game's ambiguity to a plot. The internal fights
are *additional* causes of the same mess, not the real cause of it.

## 9. Review checklist

Before shipping any faction-voiced text:

- [ ] Would the speaker's own institution consider this line accurate?
- [ ] Does it use only its own faction's lexicon for the others?
- [ ] Is the worst thing in it stated in the fewest possible words?
- [ ] Does it avoid telling the audience how to feel?
- [ ] Could you tell which faction wrote it with the attribution removed?
- [ ] Does it avoid comedy at the expense of civilians, atrocity, or the dead?
- [ ] Does it leave the moral question open?
